## 2. Data Sources and Data Types

A Customer Relationship Management System is, at its core, an information system designed to capture and consolidate every point of contact a business has with a customer — what Laudon and Laudon (2022) describe as unifying "front office" functions such as sales, marketing, and customer service around a single customer record. For Top End Explorer Travels, this means the CRMS must be deliberately designed to absorb three categories of data that currently sit in disconnected silos (spreadsheets, paper notes, and email): **structured**, **semi-structured**, and **unstructured** data. Each category has different implications for how the system should be modelled, stored, and retrieved, and a 100-level implementation plan needs to treat them as distinct architectural concerns rather than a single "customer database."

### 2.1 Structured Data

Structured data is information that conforms to a fixed schema and can be stored in relational database tables with defined fields, data types, and keys (Laudon & Laudon, 2022). For Top End Explorer Travels, the structured layer of the CRMS would be built around a normalised relational schema, for example:

- A **Client** table (`client_id` [primary key], first name, surname, date of birth, passport number, passport expiry, nationality, phone, email, postal address, emergency contact).
- A **Booking** table (`booking_id`, `client_id` [foreign key], destination, package type — domestic Kakadu/Litchfield safari vs. international itinerary, travel dates, number of travellers, booking status).
- A **Payment/Transaction Log** table (`transaction_id`, `booking_id`, amount, currency, payment method, deposit vs. balance, date, reconciliation status).
- A **Pricing Tier** table (`tier_id`, supplier, route or package, season, base rate, commission rate, validity window).

Fields such as passport numbers and payment details are classified as sensitive personal information and must be stored with column-level encryption and accessed only through role-based access control (RBAC), rather than in a plain spreadsheet cell as is currently the case — a direct architectural response to the "manual, unintegrated" problem identified in Section 1. Structured data is what makes the system *queryable*: it is what allows a consultant to search "all clients travelling to Kakadu in July" or "all outstanding balances over 30 days" in seconds rather than manually scanning spreadsheet tabs.

### 2.2 Semi-Structured Data

Semi-structured data has some organisational structure (tags, key–value pairs, or markup) but does not fit neatly into fixed relational columns, typically arriving as XML or JSON (Laudon & Laudon, 2022). In the CRMS for Top End Explorer Travels, this category includes:

- **Website inquiry forms**, submitted as JSON payloads via the agency's website and ingested into the CRMS through a webhook/API connector rather than being manually re-typed by staff.
- **Automated booking confirmation emails** generated by airlines, hotels, and tour operators, which can be parsed using a mail-parsing rule set (matching sender domain and subject-line patterns) to auto-populate booking status fields.
- **Supplier rate sheets and availability feeds**, delivered as XML (e.g., IATA NDC-style airline feeds) or JSON feeds from hotel aggregators such as HotelBeds or Expedia TAAP. These feeds update pricing-tier and availability data on a scheduled (e.g., nightly batch) or near-real-time (webhook-triggered) basis through a lightweight ETL/middleware layer sitting between the supplier API and the CRMS database.

Architecturally, semi-structured data is best handled by landing it first in a staging area (a document-oriented store or JSON column type supported by most modern relational databases, e.g., PostgreSQL's `jsonb`), and only the fields relevant to day-to-day operations (confirmed price, confirmed dates, supplier reference number) are normalised into the structured Booking and Pricing Tier tables. This two-step approach keeps the system flexible to supplier feed changes without requiring a schema migration every time an airline changes its API format.

### 2.3 Unstructured Data

Unstructured data has no predefined format and includes free text, audio, or narrative content (Laudon & Laudon, 2022). For a boutique agency whose value proposition is personalised service, this is arguably the most commercially valuable — and currently the most poorly managed — data category, including:

- **Agent–client phone call logs and consultation notes**, currently scribbled on paper, covering traveller preferences such as dietary restrictions, health or mobility needs, and seating/accommodation style.
- **Special-occasion markers** (anniversaries, honeymoons, milestone birthdays) noted informally by agents who happen to remember a particular client.
- **Post-trip feedback and review text**, whether collected via survey, email, or verbally.

Because this data has no fixed structure, it cannot simply be dumped into a spreadsheet cell and remain usable. In the CRMS, unstructured content should be captured as a **Notes/Interaction** record linked by `client_id` (so every call note, dietary flag, or feedback comment is attached to the correct customer profile rather than existing as an orphaned paper note), with a small set of controlled tags (e.g., "dietary," "mobility," "special occasion," "complaint") applied at the point of entry so that even free text becomes partially searchable and reportable. This is the specific mechanism by which the system converts today's "lost inquiries and delayed follow-ups" (Section 1) into retrievable institutional memory rather than knowledge that exists only in one agent's head or one paper notebook.

### 2.4 Summary

| Data category | Examples at Top End Explorer Travels | Primary capture mechanism | Storage approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structured | Client profiles, bookings, payments, pricing tiers | CRMS front-end forms, point-of-sale/payment gateway | Normalised relational tables, encrypted sensitive fields, RBAC |
| Semi-structured | Web inquiry forms, confirmation emails, supplier XML/JSON feeds | API/webhook integration, scheduled ETL sync | JSON staging store, then normalised into core tables |
| Unstructured | Call notes, dietary/health notes, occasion markers, feedback | Agent data entry at point of contact, post-trip survey | Tagged Notes/Interaction table linked to client ID |

Treating these three categories separately — rather than as one undifferentiated "customer data" blob — is what turns the CRMS from a digital filing cabinet into an integration platform capable of absorbing every channel through which a client currently reaches the agency.

## 3. Why Customer Information is Important

The value of consolidating this data is best explained using the distinction, common in the CRM literature, between **operational**, **analytical**, and **collaborative** CRM (Buttle & Maklan, 2019; Laudon & Laudon, 2022). Each layer maps closely onto the three stakeholder groups identified for Top End Explorer Travels — administration, sales/customer-facing staff, and external suppliers — which is the structure used below.

### 3.1 Administrative Efficiency (Operational CRM)

Operational CRM automates the routine, transaction-level front-office processes that staff currently perform manually. For Top End Explorer Travels this directly targets the problems described in Section 1: client files spread across spreadsheet tabs, paper notes, and personal email inboxes. With a centralised CRMS:

- A client record lookup that currently requires searching multiple spreadsheets and a filing tray is reduced to a single search on name, booking reference, or phone number.
- Invoice and payment tracking is automated by linking the Payment/Transaction table to the Booking table, automatically flagging overdue balances instead of relying on an administrator manually cross-checking a ledger against a booking sheet.
- Every record modification is logged against a staff login, creating an audit trail that supports both administrative accountability and reconciliation at end-of-month.

The business value here is quantifiable: administrative overhead per booking (time spent locating a file, re-entering duplicate data, chasing a payment) is reduced, and the risk of a lost inquiry — currently a direct consequence of information being trapped in an individual agent's inbox — is largely eliminated because every inbound inquiry is logged against a client record the moment it is received.

### 3.2 Customer Intimacy and Revenue Growth (Analytical CRM)

Analytical CRM uses the consolidated customer data (including the unstructured preference and feedback data discussed in Section 2.3) to generate insight that supports personalisation and sales. For a boutique agency whose competitive advantage against large online travel agents is precisely its personal relationship with clients, this is the layer with the greatest strategic value:

- Agents can instantly recall a returning client's preferred seat type, hotel style, dietary needs, and past itineraries without relying on memory or a scribbled note, enabling genuinely personalised itinerary recommendations rather than generic packages.
- Because every past booking is stored in structured form, the agency can perform basic customer segmentation (e.g., recency–frequency–monetary analysis) to identify high-value repeat clients for loyalty treatment, and to identify lapsed clients who may be worth a proactive re-engagement call.
- Cross-selling and up-selling (e.g., offering a Kakadu add-on to a client who has previously booked only international travel, or suggesting a wedding-anniversary package based on an occasion marker) becomes something the system can prompt, rather than something that depends entirely on one agent's personal recollection of one client.

This directly answers the feedback that previous work was too descriptive about "why data matters" in the abstract: the mechanism by which customer information increases revenue at Top End Explorer Travels is specifically the conversion of informal, agent-held knowledge into a searchable, system-held asset that any staff member can act on, improving both personalisation and continuity of service when a client's usual agent is unavailable.

### 3.3 Supplier Coordination (Collaborative CRM)

Collaborative CRM extends the customer data outward to external partners — in this case, the airlines, hotels, and Top-End tour operators that Top End Explorer Travels books on behalf of its clients. Because booking and availability data from suppliers arrives as semi-structured feeds (Section 2.2), integrating it into the same CRMS that holds client records allows:

- Real-time (or near-real-time) matching of confirmed client bookings against supplier availability, reducing the risk of over-booking a Kakadu tour vehicle or a hotel room that has already sold out through a different channel.
- Consolidated visibility of booking volume by supplier, which gives the agency a stronger negotiating position on commission rates with airlines and hotel partners than would be possible from scattered spreadsheet records.
- Fewer missed-coordination incidents (e.g., a confirmed flight change not being reflected in a client's accommodation booking), because both the client-facing booking record and the supplier confirmation live in the same system rather than in separate emails.

In short, customer information is not valuable to Top End Explorer Travels merely because "more data is good" — a criticism levelled at surface-level treatments of this topic — but because each stakeholder group extracts a specific, identifiable class of value from it: administration gains **efficiency and accountability**, sales/agents gain **personalisation and revenue growth**, and suppliers gain **coordination and negotiating leverage**. The CRMS architecture described in Section 2 is what makes all three possible simultaneously, from a single underlying customer record.
